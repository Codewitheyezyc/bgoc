# Document 4 — User Psychology
### Beverly Group of Companies (BGOC)

## How the user is actually thinking, before they open the app

Someone opening BGOC for the first time isn't thinking "I want to try a new super-app." They're thinking something much smaller and more local: *"I need dinner tonight,"* or *"I need a birthday gift for my niece,"* or *"I saw Beverly Bakeries has good bread, let me check if they deliver."* The app has to meet people at that small, specific need — not try to impress them with how many stores it has before they've even solved their first errand.

This matters because it shapes the whole psychology of the app: **people don't arrive loyal to "BGOC." They arrive loyal to one store, or curious about one need.** The platform earns the rest.

## The five stages of the journey

**1. Discovery — "Wait, this is Beverly's app?"**
The person hears about the app through a store they already trust — a sign at Pinnacle Mall, a post from Beverly Bakeries' page, a friend mentioning "I ordered pizza through the Beverly app." The emotional core here is *recognition*, not novelty. If the app doesn't immediately connect to a name they already know, this stage fails before it starts.

**2. First open — "Okay, what is this exactly?"**
This is the make-or-break moment. Within seconds, the person needs to understand: this is one app, seven real stores, all from the group they already know. Confusion here (unclear navigation, unclear what's a "store" vs. a "product," unclear pricing) creates the single biggest drop-off risk in the whole product.

**3. First order — "Let's see if this actually works."**
The person is testing the app, not the group. They already trust Beverly; what they don't yet trust is *this specific app's* ability to take their money, get the order right, and deliver on time. Every friction point here — a confusing checkout, an unclear delivery estimate, a payment that doesn't feel secure — reads as "the app is unreliable," even if the store itself is excellent.

**4. Second store — "Oh, I didn't know they also had toys."**
This is the moment the whole platform strategy either pays off or doesn't. If the app makes it natural to notice Dollnatia or Celebrity Styles while checking out from Yurmealicious Pizza, this is where BGOC becomes more valuable than any single store alone. If the app doesn't surface other stores at all, it's just seven apps wearing one skin — and the platform bet fails quietly.

**5. Habit — "I just check the Beverly app first."**
The person now defaults to BGOC before thinking of anywhere else for food, gifts, home items, or clothing within its categories. This is loyalty to the *platform*, not just to one store — and it's the payoff of everything before it working well.

## Fears, spoken and unspoken

- **"Will my order actually arrive, and correctly?"** — the most basic fear with any local delivery app, especially a new one without years of reviews behind it yet.
- **"Is my money safe here?"** — payment trust matters more for a newer app than an established one; people are more cautious the first time they enter card details.
- **"Am I buying from the real Beverly, or something using their name?"** — because the brand is locally known, people will (correctly) be alert to knockoffs or confusion, so the app must make its legitimacy obvious immediately.
- **"If something's wrong with my order, who do I even talk to?"** — especially tricky with seven different stores; people fear getting bounced between "that's a Beverly Bakeries issue" and "that's an app issue" with no one taking responsibility.

## What motivates them

- **Convenience that respects their time** — one place instead of five different WhatsApp threads or physical trips across Pinnacle Mall.
- **Trust already earned** — they're not being asked to trust a stranger; they're being asked to trust Beverly Group in a new format.
- **Discovery without effort** — finding out "oh, they also sell X" feels like a pleasant surprise, not an upsell, if done well.
- **Feeling like a known customer, not a number** — order history, saved details, and a wallet that remembers them make the app feel personal rather than transactional.

## How the product should speak to these feelings

- At first open: **orient before impressing.** A short, simple visual — "One app. Every Beverly Group store." — grounds the person before showing them anything else.
- During checkout: **overcommunicate reliability**, not with jargon, but with plain confirmations — "Your order is confirmed," "On its way," "Delivered" — the same way a trusted shopkeeper would reassure a customer face-to-face.
- When surfacing a second store: **suggest, don't sell.** "You might also like Homeworld" lands better than a banner ad — it should feel like a shopkeeper pointing next door, not a marketing push.
- When something goes wrong: **one clear point of contact**, regardless of which store the issue is with — because "who do I even talk to" is one of the sharpest fears above, and the app answering it clearly builds more trust than almost any other single feature.
